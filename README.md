## What Is Considered a Dental Emergency?

A dental emergency is generally a problem that involves significant pain, bleeding, swelling, infection, trauma, or damage that may worsen without timely treatment. Severe toothache, a knocked-out permanent tooth, a fractured tooth, a dental abscess, uncontrolled oral bleeding, or swelling around the face and jaw can all require prompt professional assessment.

Not every dental problem requires emergency treatment. A minor chip without pain, for example, may be suitable for a regular appointment. However, persistent or increasing pain should not be ignored simply because there is no visible damage. A dental examination can determine whether the issue involves decay, infection, trauma, or another underlying condition.

## Why Sudden Tooth Pain Should Not Be Ignored

Tooth pain can have several causes, including cavities, cracked teeth, gum infections, exposed dental nerves, or an abscess. The CDC reports that about **21% of U.S. adults ages 20 to 64 have at least one untreated cavity**. Untreated decay can cause pain and infection and may eventually contribute to an abscess.

Pain that becomes severe, lasts for an extended period, or is accompanied by swelling deserves particular attention. Waiting for symptoms to disappear may allow an underlying problem to progress. Professional evaluation is especially important when pain interferes with sleeping, eating, speaking, or normal daily activities.

## When Swelling May Indicate an Infection

Swelling around a tooth, gum, cheek, or jaw can be associated with infection. An abscess is a pocket of infection that can develop around a tooth or within the surrounding tissues. The CDC notes that untreated cavities can lead to abscesses, and severe infections can spread beyond the mouth.

Warning signs can include:

* Increasing facial or jaw swelling
* Severe or throbbing tooth pain
* Sensitivity to hot or cold
* A bad taste or drainage in the mouth
* Fever or feeling unwell
* Difficulty opening the mouth
* Difficulty swallowing or breathing

Difficulty breathing or swallowing, rapidly increasing facial swelling, or signs of a serious systemic infection require urgent medical attention. In such situations, an emergency department may be appropriate, particularly if airway problems are present.

## What to Do After a Broken or Knocked-Out Tooth

Dental injuries can occur during sports, falls, accidents, or other unexpected events. If a permanent tooth is knocked out, time is important. Handle the tooth carefully by the crown rather than the root, avoid scrubbing it, and seek professional dental care as quickly as possible.

For a broken tooth, avoid chewing on the affected side and keep any broken pieces if they can be safely collected. Rinsing the mouth gently with water can help remove debris. If there is bleeding, clean gauze can be applied with gentle pressure.

A damaged tooth does not necessarily need to be removed. Depending on the injury, a dentist may determine that restoration or another treatment can preserve the tooth.

Access to appropriate dental care is important when oral pain becomes severe. According to the American Dental Association, there are more than **2 million hospital emergency-department visits each year in the United States for dental pain**. The ADA also explains that hospital emergency departments generally cannot provide comprehensive dental treatment because most do not have dentists on staff.

This distinction matters because pain medication or antibiotics may address certain symptoms temporarily without correcting the dental problem itself. A dental professional can examine the affected area, identify the underlying cause, and discuss appropriate treatment.

For people in Charlotte, choosing care based on the urgency of the symptoms can help avoid unnecessary delays. When possible, patients should provide details about their symptoms, when they began, and whether swelling, trauma, fever, or bleeding is present.

## How to Prepare for an Urgent Dental Visit

Before seeking urgent dental care, gather relevant information about medications, allergies, existing dental conditions, and recent injuries. If possible, bring identification and insurance information. Patients should also describe the onset and severity of their symptoms accurately.

Do not place aspirin directly on a painful tooth or gum because it can irritate oral tissues. For temporary comfort, follow appropriate over-the-counter medication directions and avoid extremely hot, cold, hard, or sugary foods if they increase sensitivity.
